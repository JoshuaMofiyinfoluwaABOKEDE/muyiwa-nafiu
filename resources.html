How to Lead a Song Session

Leading a session is a sample of a larger flow, and it deserves careful planning. The call to worship, usually the first few words you speak before a song, should set the proper tone for the whole session. Some leaders resort to hypes and gingers, which are really tactics to get people to respond. You can be innovative, but be realistic about it. People do not receive their neighbour’s blessing in church by shouting louder hallelujahs.

Read the full article

If the leader before you has done a good job of setting the tone, it is wise to flow along that tide rather than starting all over. Every service leader has one job: Jesus glorified and saints edified. Many times you will find your own flow as the service progresses, so there is no need to be deliberately late. Be open to spontaneous leadings, but do not be reckless. Anything that rises in your mind on stage should be tested against God’s word before you act on it.

Close with a clear sense of time. Many people use more time than necessary under the guise of ‘the songs keep coming’. If you are good at what you do, the songs will keep coming, but it is your job to put a lid on it when your time is up. You are not the only one who hears God in the assembly. If time does not permit you to give a message, trust the Spirit of God to inspire it through another vessel.

Try this

  1. Write your session as three parts before Sunday: an entry of two or three songs, a body with room to adjust, and a closing you can deliver in under a minute.
  2. During the session, glance at the room every few songs and decide whether to hold, move on or slow down.
  3. After the session, note which transition felt smooth and which felt abrupt, and fix the abrupt one next time.

How to Choose a Key the Room Can Sing

During congregational sessions, scaling, scatting, freestyles and other professional touches should be kept to a minimum. They can easily shift the attention of the church from God to you, and you do not want to steal God’s spotlight. Choose a key on which the congregation can sing comfortably. When your voice is too loud, many people will leave the singing to you alone, and the room stops being a congregation.

Read the full article

It is alright to change the responses of a few songs. But when the session becomes all about innovation, attention moves from God to the leader, and that has become the norm in many places. Congregational worship should be made as simple and as relatable as possible. Syncopations may be done, but they should be minimal and, where possible, inconspicuous.

Musicians may want an instrumental interlude within the session. You may give it to them, but within a reasonable scope. Before you settle on a key, ask whether the people in the room can join in. A key that suits only the soloist does not serve the room.

Try this

  1. Sing the melody at your planned key while sitting in the back of the room or in a quiet space, imagining the least confident voice in the congregation.
  2. Keep a simple list of keys that worked for your church and the songs they suited.
  3. If a key is marginal, drop it by a step rather than pushing the room to reach.

What to Do Before You Minister

A mind that is robust in knowledge and experience will interpret the inspiration of the Holy Spirit better. A worship leader should have a deep well of words to draw from. If you only know the chorus or refrain of a song, you are no better than any other member of the congregation. A song leader should know many songs and be vast in lyrics, which will also be useful in prophetic moments when you need a fuller pool of encouragement, exhortation or comfort.

Read the full article

You do not need to wait for an assignment before you prepare. Arrange songs in your personal time and keep some of them in your heart for impromptu moments. Keep a personal journal of song arrangements, because you can be called to function without prior notice. Keep a notepad close by, and let it look serious rather than a jotter that slips out of the pocket. It is a sign that you believe God speaks.

Pray for your church and your pastor. If you make a habit of praying for the church, you will experience the leading of the Lord in surprising ways, and He will put His honour upon your ministry in that assembly. Keep friends around you who are ministry-minded, love-minded and Christ-minded. Your congregation can read you like a book, so let your preparation be real.

Try this

  1. Set aside fifteen minutes a day for scripture and listening, and write one thing you noticed.
  2. Start a single notebook or phone note titled with the date, and record any arrangement or impression you want to keep.
  3. Pray for three named people in your congregation before every service you lead.

How to Dress for the Platform

The day you lead in worship may be the wrong time to try on clothing that demands attention, or an accessory you are not used to, such as heels, a wig or sneakers. In any sane worship attire, you should be able to lift your hands, bend, kneel and even jump without tripping or revealing parts of your body that should stay covered. Test your outfit before the service by doing the movements you will need.

Read the full article

Heavy dressing and heavy make-up can distract a room from the purpose of the gathering. When leaders dress to be noticed, the congregation may not be expecting a serious move of the Spirit. Your appearance should serve the worship rather than compete with it. Choose footwear you can stand in for the whole session without discomfort.

Christian musicians are not required to dress and speak like their non-Christian counterparts. The styles that are common in some genres are comfortable habits created by earlier performers, not rules of engagement. Ask a trusted mentor for honest feedback, and be willing to adjust so that people see Christ rather than your outfit.

Try this

  1. Before your next service, practise kneeling, lifting your hands and moving between instruments in the outfit you plan to wear.
  2. Ask one trusted person whether anything in your look draws attention away from the worship.
  3. Choose shoes you can stand in for ninety minutes without needing to sit down.

How to Introduce a New Song

A worship session is not the place to introduce every song you have written. It is a congregational time of adoration, so the congregation should be considered when choosing songs. If a new song is to be done, let the lyrics be projected or printed so that people can relate to them quickly. The church should be helped to take part in the singing rather than left to watch the team perform.

Read the full article

If people are standing for minutes watching you and your team, then the session has not been done well. A new song works when people can join it, and it becomes a burden when they can only observe it. Practise the song with your team until it feels natural, so that the room is not left waiting while you find your footing.

Be patient if the first attempt does not land. Revisit the song in later weeks, ask your team how it is feeling, and do not give up on it after one awkward Sunday. The songs that last often took time to take root, and their staying power is part of their value.

Try this

  1. Sing the new song through with your team at least three times before you teach it to the congregation.
  2. Give the congregation one sentence on why the song matters, then sing the chorus so they can hear it.
  3. Plan to repeat the song in the next two or three weeks before judging whether it has taken root.

Watch Your Diction

The mannerisms in your enunciation can be endearing or off-putting. Anointed ministers have been heard pronouncing the name of Jesus in a way that distracts from the name itself, and other common words have picked up small, unintended additions. A stray letter here and there can turn worship into something else entirely. Your diction matters because the congregation learns the words the way you sing them.

Read the full article

To improve your diction, make a habit of listening to your recordings. You may be surprised to learn that you do not sound as polished as you think. Practise difficult words slowly before you lead them, and look up the correct pronunciation of names, places and terms, especially in songs drawn from other languages or cultures.

Clarity is a form of care for the text you are singing. A word sung clearly and repeated in a session can become the way a whole congregation says it for years. Ask a trusted listener to point out the words they could not understand, and make small, consistent corrections.

Try this

  1. Look up the correct pronunciation of every name, place and foreign word in your set before rehearsal.
  2. Record one rehearsal on your phone and listen back for swallowed endings and stretched vowels.
  3. Ask one listener to point out the three words they could not understand.

How to Evaluate Yourself After Ministering

After a session, repent of any strange expression you noticed while leading, and address any trace of temptation. Repentance is not the same as condemnation, so never condemn yourself. After an honest evaluation, give thanks for the privilege of leading God’s people. Reflection should build you up rather than wear you down.

Read the full article

Ask focused questions. Did the opening draw people in? Did the transitions flow? Did I listen to the leading of the Spirit, or only to my own plan? Write down two or three concrete changes for next time rather than a long list of everything that went wrong. A short, clear list is far easier to act on.

Then return to learning. The leader who keeps learning has more to draw on when the next session changes direction. Share your reflections with a trusted team member and invite honest feedback. Others often notice what you cannot see from the front.

Try this

  1. Answer three questions in writing: what worked, what felt forced and what I will change next time.
  2. Limit yourself to two concrete changes for the next session.
  3. Ask one trusted person for one piece of honest feedback within the week.

How to Brief Your Background Vocalists

If you are a backup vocalist, a few things matter most. Blend your voice with the other backup singers so that your own voice is not easily picked out. Be as lively as the lead vocalist, because when you are on backup duty you do not have a mood of your own. Your energy should match the leader’s.

Read the full article

Be ready to cover without taking the stage. You should be as prayed up as the lead vocalist, or you will keep dragging down the strength of the team. Learn your songs well, so that you can be a good backbone for the song leader and stay steady when the leader raises a song that was not rehearsed.

The leader should brief the team clearly before the service: the key, the song order, the entry points and the purpose of each moment. Agree on one simple visual cue for changes and rehearse it once. A team that knows the plan sounds like one voice.

Try this

  1. Send the key, the song order and the entry points to the team the day before the service.
  2. Agree on one visual cue for changes and rehearse it once.
  3. End the briefing with a short prayer and a reminder of what the moment is for.

Be Time-Conscious Onstage

Many people use more time than necessary under the guise of ‘the songs keep coming’. If you are good at what you do, the songs will keep coming, but it is your job to put a lid on it when your time is up. Respecting the schedule is a form of care for the people who come after you and for the pastor who planned the programme.

Read the full article

Time-sensitive leading matters. Some instructions have an expiry window, after which the result of obedience may no longer be what it should have been. Excellence is good, but promptness is superior to excellence. While you strive to do a thing well, try to do it on the clock.

Arrive early enough to get seated and attend the preparatory prayers. Pressure and disorderliness are major setbacks to the flow of the anointing. Calm preparation produces calm leadership, and people notice the difference.

Try this

  1. Write the planned start and end time of your session on the card you keep on the platform.
  2. Agree with the pastor on a discreet signal for when you need to wrap up.
  3. Arrive at least thirty minutes early to set up, test equipment and pray.

What Actually Counts as Service

Real ministry is a result of your compassion, and not of your need. If it comes from compassion, you will spend and be spent. If it comes from your need, you will always make demands. Ministry is an expression of compassion. It is not primarily a way to provide food and clothes for the minister. It is primarily a way to show people that God is deeply in love with them.

Read the full article

Every true minister of God is a love letter from God to people. These letters will not discriminate between the rich and the poor, will not put their own needs above the assignment, and will not choose whom to love and whom to despise. Let ministry come from true love and compassion, and not from the desperate need to make ends meet.

Sonship and service are not opposites. They are two complementary sides of the same coin. The souls of men are God’s most prized possessions. When ministers live with a constant awareness of the worth of souls, every other part of christian service falls into its proper place.

Try this

  1. This week, do one unseen act of care for someone in your music team and tell no one about it.
  2. Ask yourself honestly whether you would still serve if your name were never mentioned.
  3. Write down the name of one person you have stopped noticing and reach out to them.

How to Tell Humility From Timidity

Timidity is seeing yourself as less than God sees you, while arrogance is seeing yourself as more. Both are forms of pride. A low self-esteem may be dressed up as humility. A fearful person may be mistaken for a gentle or quiet one, while their inspiration is continually buried under their perception of other people’s perfection.

Read the full article

Humility is seeing yourself the way God sees you, no more and no less. If God says you are forgiven and you disagree, that is not humility but a sign of oppression. Inferiority is believing your failures above God’s word, and superiority is believing your privileges above God’s word. Whatever God’s word says about you, believe it.

Even humility comes in levels, so stay honest with yourself. Ask trusted people where you are too cautious and where you are too sure of yourself. Then take steps in the right direction, offering your ideas to the team while making space for others to lead.

Try this

  1. Name one area where you have been too cautious and one where you have been too confident.
  2. Offer one idea to your team in the next rehearsal, even if it is not your own song.
  3. Give a sincere public word of thanks to someone whose gift you admire.

How to Handle Envy in the Music Team

When people celebrate you for something, others will come who do the same thing like you, or better than you, and people will celebrate them too. You cannot afford to build your identity on these expressions, because they are not strong enough to carry you for long. Your mind must be rooted in firmer truths, so that when others come along who are strong in your area, you can receive them as complementers rather than competitors.

Read the full article

Competitive attitudes come from a lack of security in your calling. When you become settled in your calling and embrace the intricacies of God’s hand upon you, competition will have little hold on you. Bitterness about someone else’s visibility often comes from one of three thoughts: you don’t know them, so you think they couldn’t deserve it; you know them, so you think they don’t deserve it; or you know yourself, so you think you deserve what they have.

Speak to yourself often, especially when you are alone. Do not fight thoughts with thoughts; overcome them with words. When envy, a competitive mind or a critical spirit shows up, deal with it at once and speak the truth over yourself until it loses its grip.

Try this

  1. When envy rises, pause and name the feeling in a sentence, then ask God what it reveals.
  2. Praise the person you envy, specifically and sincerely, within the next day.
  3. Write down what you are grateful for in your own assignment, not in comparison to anyone else.

There’s No Small Work in God’s House

‘There is no little work of God.’ Whatever the Lord instructs is a big deal, even when it does not gather clout. Simple work is anything that does not make media waves, does not appeal to multitudes, and may not be the desire of an immature christian. The servant of the Lord should be content to run these errands, even if they earn no applause on this side of eternity.

Read the full article

The faithful person handles no work carelessly, because to them every spiritual work is sacred. When there is an opportunity to go out for evangelism, go. When the chairs need washing or the church needs rearranging, be there. Grace is not only released through prayer and rehearsal; there are other necessary parts of a genuinely anointed life, and many of them are unglamorous.

Small faithfulness often leads to larger responsibility. The person who tends the details is often the one entrusted with bigger things later. Do the small task as an act of worship, and people will notice the consistency long before they notice your talent.

Try this

  1. Volunteer for one task this month that nobody else wants, such as setting up chairs or checking cables.
  2. Do it without announcing it, and notice how it changes your attitude toward the rest of your service.
  3. Thank the person who usually does that task, specifically.

Worship Leading Is Warfare, Not Performance

Praise worship leading is warfare. Be conscious of the spiritual battle as you lead, and be conscious also that you are warring from a place of victory. Speak in tongues if you perceive lukewarmness in the room. Declare the scriptures and speak in psalms. There will be many days when you need to muster the warrior within you, who will not cower at the face of a good challenge.

Read the full article

Many times there are spiritual contentions for the attention of people in meetings. You must be sensitive to know that everything does not always begin and end with singing. Gospel singers must be versatile in spiritual intelligence and warfare. When a congregation is unresponsive, pray before you sing, and pray with the atmosphere in mind rather than the setlist.

Do not be intimidated by unfriendly faces in the room. Not every frown means the Spirit is absent, and people who feel no conviction may react strongly to tongues or scripture. Do not shrink in the face of a good fight of faith. Worship is not a performance judged by the room; it is a response to God that also shapes the spiritual climate of the gathering.

Try this

  1. Pray for the pastor, the people and the service for ten minutes before you lead.
  2. During the service, notice one shift in the atmosphere and give God the glory silently.
  3. Afterwards, thank someone who prayed for you during the week.

Guard Against the Entitlement Mindset

People are often frustrated and bitter today because they feel entitled to what others have: the global acceptance, the wealth, the pomp and the spotlight. Musicians can slip into believing they are owed a salary, a gadget allowance or a title. When expectations are never spoken and never met, they quietly sour a ministry and make every small disappointment feel like a grievance.

Read the full article

May you be delivered from the entitlement mindset. May you come to see yourself as a giver and not just a receiver of gifts and honorariums. Do not give up your equipment or your time based on assumptions. Let there be clear agreements about the use of your personal instruments, so that no one is left guessing and no one feels cheated.

Gratitude keeps your hands open rather than clenched. Thank the people who provide instruments, transport and meals, even when it is part of their normal role. An open hand is far easier to fill with blessing, and it protects you from the bitterness that quietly ends many promising ministries.

Try this

  1. Make a list of ten people who make your ministry possible and thank each one this week.
  2. When you feel owed something, pause and pray for the person you think owes it to you.
  3. Decide in advance how you will respond to a decision you disagree with.

What to Do When You’re Overlooked

A minister may be faithful and never be famous. Fame is not the reward for faithfulness. Fame is simply an opportunity to reach more people. It is not a sign of a higher degree of faithfulness. If you are not famous, you are not being punished. Where you are is not nowhere. In God’s agenda, you are not in the middle of nowhere, and being overlooked by people is not being overlooked by God.

Read the full article

Keep working in your space as if your life depends on it. This is faithfulness. Stay committed, be there always, and your questions will be answered. Lead the children’s choir, or the teens’ choir, or the worship team, as if nothing else matters. Obscurity with men is not obscurity with God; He sees.

If you are waiting to succeed before you do your best, you may hardly ever succeed. Success in any sphere belongs to people who consistently do their best, whether or not anyone is watching. It is better to teach your best songs before you are famous than to wait for a platform that may never come.

Try this

  1. Write down what you feel when you are overlooked, then bring that list to God in prayer.
  2. Ask what skill or character God may be developing in you during this season.
  3. Serve one person this week in a way that asks nothing back.

Serve From Sonship, Not Fear

The sacred truth is that the One in whose presence you stand to lead His people in songs of praise, prayer and adoration is not an ordinary authority. He is your Father. That truth gives confidence: you are not a dog, and you are not a beggar. This is your Father’s house, and you belong here.

Read the full article

It also makes you open. You will not be faking anything. In your father’s house, you can come out without your wig or your makeup, and you can eat without pretence. Mothers know that it is abnormal for a child to hide in his father’s house. When a child hides there, it is a sign that something has gone wrong.

Your identity as someone called is different from basing your identity on the expression of your gifts. If you derive your identity from how you express your gifts, you may soon be disappointed, because there will always be people who do it better. Your place in God’s work was prepared for you, and you can stand in it with peace.

Try this

  1. Begin rehearsal with a short prayer that reminds you of your place in God’s household.
  2. Ask a team member for help with one task you would normally handle alone.
  3. Celebrate one small win with your team at the end of the session.

Numbers Were Never the Measure

Some music ministers will lead congregations of thousands, some will lead churches of hundreds, and some will minister to congregations of tens. Each one must see themselves as the Lord’s minstrel, without being intimidated by numbers and without seeing their church as a stepping stone to greater heights. Faithfulness in a small room is not less valuable than faithfulness on a large stage.

Read the full article

In many places, how much honour a minister receives is measured by the number of streams on a project. You must guard your heart from this carnal reasoning. The joy of ministry should not be hinged on numbers. The Lord can spend so much to reach just a handful of people, because in His kingdom the souls of men are the most priced possessions.

Measure your ministry by love and by faithfulness. Stay steady, always walking in honour and always full of love, and remember that the people in front of you matter to God whether they are ten or ten thousand.

Try this

  1. Pick three people in your church or community and pray for each by name this week.
  2. Stop checking your numbers for one day and notice how you feel.
  3. Invest in one skill that will serve your congregation more than your audience.

Faithful and Unknown Is Still a Win

There is a subtle temptation to serve in the local church with the suspicion that the Lord will soon make you famous if you are only faithful. That mentality can be one definition of unfaithfulness, because it leaves people frustrated when they do not see the result they want for a long time. The truth is that a minister may be faithful and never be famous.

Read the full article

The hidden years are often where the deepest roots grow. The Lord’s track record shows that He can spend a great deal to reach just a handful of people, because in His eyes a single soul is precious. Your reward comes from the Lord, not from the size of your crowd. Fame is not the reward; the Lord is the reward.

Stay where you have been planted, and keep serving with excellence. Faithful work in a small place prepares a minister for whatever comes next, and God decides the timing better than we do.

Try this

  1. Write down three faithful things you have done that no one publicly recognised.
  2. Give thanks for one person whose service has never been noticed.
  3. Commit to one unseen act of service in the coming week.

The Doctrine of Fame

It is hard to find a skilled musician who has never received a fame prophecy. Honest people may claim to have seen a golden crown on your head, or other signs that you are on your way to a global stage. When words like great, world, big, power and anointed appear in prophetic utterances, there is a tendency to read them through what is prevalent in our society, without giving authority to the patterns in God’s word.

Read the full article

The unspoken mantra of today’s church culture is ‘if you are not famous, you are not powerful’. Fame has become both an idol and a beast in the present-day gospel music space. Fame is not a general promise to every person in ministry, music or not. But if the Lord puts a word of visibility in your spirit, own it with confidence, because it is a gift of greater responsibility and a platform for greater impact.

The fulfilment of God’s promises is perfectly spaced to allow the dealings needed for the preservation and longevity of the man of God. The timing of each stage is regulated to prepare you for what the Lord has prepared for you. Hold fame loosely, and let God be the only reason for your ministry.

Try this

  1. Review your last three choices and ask whether each was shaped by the audience or by your conscience.
  2. Ask a trusted friend to tell you one thing about your public life that needs correcting.
  3. Set a boundary on how much time you spend on platforms that measure your worth.

The Three Stages of Every Gift

The beginning should be the shortest stage in anyone’s life. At this stage a person’s movement is like that of the proverbial crab: the first few steps are a confusing sight, and you may not be able to tell its direction. You are a bundle of many gifts and much confusion. Serving in the church will help cut away the excesses and teach you focus and discipline.

Read the full article

The imitation stage comes next. Every genuine minister is a bundle of the impressions other ministers have made on them, in doctrine, mannerisms, dressing, and the presentation of thoughts. Such impressions register in your subconscious through exposure, and they come out without special effort. It is not an insult to your anointing to act like your father in the faith or your mentor. If anyone asks, it is a mark of true followership.

The maturing stage is where you deal more with understanding and inspiration. You begin to discover the hand of God upon you in a specific pattern, because the hand of God moves upon people in traceable patterns. You may be inspired by an album in another language and still write many songs in your own. Enjoy every stage of growth, because time is the currency of mastery.

Try this

  1. Name which stage you are in for your main gift: beginning, imitation or maturing.
  2. If you are imitating, write down why the technique you copy works.
  3. Choose one way to stretch your own voice this month.

How to Find Your Niche Without Being Boxed In

A niche is a term borrowed from ecology, but for us it refers to the rhythm of grace upon a person, the mode in which a musician expresses a gift from God. Simply put, it is your strong point, the way God expresses Himself through you. Your niche is not an improvement on the gospel. It is not an addition to the gospel, and it is not an innovation on the gospel. The gospel is already a perfect story and needs no upgrade.

Read the full article

There is a difference between a niche and a stereotype. A niche is the product of freedom of expression in your spirit. A stereotype is the product of being stuck, whether to past successes, a particular culture, or the avoidance of failure. If people tag you by one kind of expression, do not let that become your only acceptable mode, because over time it can make you unable to be taken seriously in anything else.

A good minister should not be only a specialist; a good minister should be a generalist. A niche should not be a cover story for laziness or an excuse for ignorance. Specialisation comes from more knowledge, and the higher you go, the more focused you should be. What you do not know how to do, you can learn, and your skill is sharpened by much learning and observation.

Try this

  1. Ask three leaders to describe the one area they most associate you with, and whether they would trust you elsewhere.
  2. Learn one new style this quarter that lies outside your comfort zone.
  3. Accept one invitation that stretches your range, even if it feels uncertain.

What to Do When Fame Finds You Early

Money and fame can meet you at any stage of growth. In our society, nonsense can become famous, a genuine gift can become famous, nonsense can be ignored, and a genuine gift may never become famous. Do not let fame or money become the reason you stop growing. Platforms of influence have met people at their crude or imitation stage, and some of those people mistook it for maturity and stopped growing. Years later they lost the fame, the money and the seed that could have matured.

Read the full article

Your anointing is not a new kingdom discovery. God trains His men using His trained men. Do not only be interested in what your mentor is doing; be interested in how long they have been doing it. The performance of anything is only a small percentage of its intricacies, so if you only watch the performance, you may not learn much. Some spiritual and intellectual investments take time to materialise. Consistency is the birthplace of effectiveness.

Be an honest person about why you started. If you approach church music as an avenue to fame and wealth, it may be hard to open yourself to God’s word and to discipleship. You may keep moving from church to church, looking for people who will invest in you, and you will never feel satisfied even if they give you all they have. Keep close to the people who invest in your character as well as your reach.

Try this

  1. Choose a mentor who has been in the public eye for at least ten years and ask them to meet you regularly.
  2. Write down your boundaries for time, money and family before the next offer arrives.
  3. Spend one hour each week in private prayer that has nothing to do with your work.

What to Do With a Prophetic Word

Music minister, whenever you receive a prophetic word concerning your life and ministry, the first thing to do is calm down and make no rash decision. The second is to approach the Lord with a sober mind in prayer about the word you received. Understand the scope of what He is saying, so that you do not run very fast on a path He never prescribed.

Read the full article

Things would be much saner if we had purer streams of utterance, not polluted by selfish desires and personal gain. The days of greater honour for the prophetic are here, when God’s holy prophets speak as inspired by the Lord and not as desired by men. God’s word gives us the right to judge prophecies, so test what you receive against the scripture.

A word can be right and still be misapplied. Keep working faithfully in the place where you are, and let the word unfold in its season rather than forcing its timing. The Lord has always been more careful with timing than we are.

Try this

  1. Write the word down with its date and the context in which you received it.
  2. Check the word against scripture and ask two trusted leaders to weigh it.
  3. Identify one faithful action this week that moves you toward the word without forcing its timing.

Hold Your Public Image Loosely

How to Write a Song — Two Ways In

There are two general ways of writing a new gospel song: by the unction and by faith. Writing by the unction is the one many people like to hear, because it looks like a way to bypass diligence, where the Spirit takes over. That is not really the case. The important thing with this type of composition is to be sensitive to the flow, because it will not stay for long, so capture it while it is there.

Read the full article

Writing by a deliberate act of faith means sitting down on purpose to write a new song for a church meeting, a conference, a concert or an album. It is not wrong for a believer to combine their musical ability with biblical knowledge and produce a new song by faith. You may not perceive any special anointing in the moment. You are simply a child of God composing a spiritual song.

Both types are subject to the training of your mind. The Holy Spirit may inspire a rich word, while a musician delivers it in a shallow way if their mind is not formed. The inspiration is what the Spirit supplies; the choice of words and the mode of delivery depend on the robustness of the writer’s mind. This is why the christian songwriter must first be full of God’s word and have a robust pool of healthy words to draw from.

  2. When inspiration comes, record it within five minutes, even if it is only a single phrase.
  3. Choose one theme each week and draft a verse and chorus from it, then edit.

Read the full article

Safety Tips for Every Songwriter

Avoid writing on subjects you have not learned well. You should have a good grasp of a subject before you attempt to write on it. Have a few people in your life who vet your writing for doctrinal balance, and listen to them even when their feedback is hard to hear.

Avoid meaningless clichés and name-calling. Many songwriters could not defend the name they called God in their song if they were asked to do so. Be careful with claims that a song was written entirely by the Holy Spirit, because that is often said to stop people questioning a song.   1. Before releasing any lyric, check each claim against scripture in your own Bible.
  2. Ask one theologically trained friend to review the song.
  3. Cut every phrase that sounds spiritual but says nothing specific.
